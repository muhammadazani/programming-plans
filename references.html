References

The ideas on this site come from decades of research on how people learn to program. Here are the sources, what each one contributes, and the design work and tools the site is built with.

01

Research behind the plans

  1. Soloway, E., & Ehrlich, K. (1984). Empirical studies of programming knowledge. IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, SE-10(5), 595–609. doi.org/10.1109/TSE.1984.5010283

    What it shows: experienced programmers recognise programming plans, stereotyped pieces of code such as a running total or a search loop, and follow the unwritten rules for combining them. Novices haven't built up that library yet.

    Used here for: the whole idea of the site. Each card is one plan, named and taught on its own.

  2. Soloway, E. (1986). Learning to program = learning to construct mechanisms and explanations. Communications of the ACM, 29(9), 850–858. doi.org/10.1145/6592.6594

    What it shows: plans, and the ways of combining them, can be taught explicitly, instead of leaving students to discover them from finished programs.

    Used here for: the language-neutral steps on every card, and the plans that are built from other plans (Average = Accumulate + Counting).

  3. Spohrer, J. C., & Soloway, E. (1986). Novice mistakes: Are the folk wisdoms correct? Communications of the ACM, 29(7), 624–632. doi.org/10.1145/6138.6145

    What it shows: most novice bugs are not misunderstandings of individual statements; a small number of bug types, many of them about putting plans together, account for a large share of the errors.

    Used here for: the Pitfalls sections and the buggy code in the quizzes: returning inside the loop, resetting a total on every pass, starting a maximum at 0.

  4. Sajaniemi, J. (2002). An empirical analysis of roles of variables in novice-level procedural programs. In Proceedings of the IEEE 2002 Symposia on Human Centric Computing Languages and Environments (HCC'02) (pp. 37–39). IEEE. doi.org/10.1109/HCC.2002.1046340

    What it shows: a small set of roles (stepper, most-wanted holder, gatherer, one-way flag, fixed value and a few more) covers almost all variables in novice programs.

    Used here for: the role labels on the variables in each animated trace: walker, counter, best so far, gatherer, stepper, fixed.

  5. Muller, O., Ginat, D., & Haberman, B. (2007). Pattern-oriented instruction and its influence on problem decomposition and solution construction. In Proceedings of the 12th Annual SIGCSE Conference on Innovation and Technology in Computer Science Education (ITiCSE '07) (pp. 151–155). ACM. doi.org/10.1145/1268784.1268830

    What it shows: students taught to recognise and apply algorithmic patterns explicitly break problems down and build solutions better than students taught without them.

    Used here for: organising the site as a catalogue, the clue words on each card, and the plan finder, which trains the step of recognising which plan a problem needs.

02

Design inspiration

These pattern catalogues shaped how the cards and the catalogue are laid out. No content was copied from them.

  • Data Cards Playbook: Patterns, Google Research.

    The do / don't tips with a one-line takeaway, used in every Pitfalls section.

  • Design Patterns Catalogue, Projects by IF.

    A catalogue grouped by category with counts, an illustration per pattern, and status labels such as “coming soon”.

  • Design Patterns, Refactoring.Guru.

    The teaching order of a pattern page (intent, problem, analogy, structure, code, relations) and explicit “don't confuse it with” comparisons.

Language documentation

The idiomatic versions on each card use these standard libraries. Their official documentation is the place to look things up.

  • Python · Built-in functions (any, all, sum, max) and itertools
  • Java · java.util.stream and String
  • Rust · Iterator and str
  • C · Null-terminated byte strings (string.h, ctype.h), cppreference.com
  • C++ · Constrained algorithms (std::ranges) and std::accumulate, cppreference.com
